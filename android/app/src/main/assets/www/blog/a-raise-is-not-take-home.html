A raise is not the amount that hits your account

A higher hourly wage raises the gross check by more than it raises take-home.

A $2 raise sounds like $2 more for every hour on the check. Gross pay does work that way. Take-home does not. Federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare, and any state tax apply to the extra dollars. The amount that reaches the account is the raise minus those lines.

Forty hours at $20, then at $22

On a weekly check, 40 hours at $20 is $800 gross. The same 40 hours at $22 is $880 gross. The raise is $80 on that check, or $4,160 over a 52-week year if the hours stay full. In this estimate, for a single filer in Pennsylvania with no local tax, no 401(k), and no health premium, take-home moves from about $660 to about $722. About $62 of the $80 shows up. The other $18 is tax. A $1 raise, from $20 to $21, is $40 more gross and about $31 more take-home on that same check.

Why the whole raise is not taxed at one rate

Social Security is 6.2 percent of the extra wages, and Medicare is 1.45 percent, until a wage hits a limit the calculator already applies. Federal income tax takes a slice of the extra taxable dollars at the bracket those dollars fall in, not a slice of the whole paycheck. Pennsylvania’s wage tax in this estimate is a flat percent on top. None of those rates is a reason to skip a raise. They are the reason a budget should use the new take-home, not the new wage times the hours.

Type the old wage and the new wage into the calculator with the same hours, filing status, and state. The gap between the two take-home numbers is the raise you can spend. It is an estimate, not a pay stub and not tax advice. The Internal Revenue Service publishes the federal pieces this estimate is drawn from.
